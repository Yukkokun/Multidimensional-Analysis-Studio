Recent research on register variation has demonstrated that linguistic features are not distributed randomly across texts.
Instead, particular combinations of features tend to co-occur in functionally similar contexts, and these co-occurring patterns have been interpreted as evidence for underlying dimensions of variation.
The present study extends this line of inquiry by examining a corpus of academic articles drawn from three disciplines.
Each text was annotated for part-of-speech categories, and approximately sixty linguistic features were computed.
Dimension scores were then derived by standardising feature frequencies against previously reported means and standard deviations.
The results indicate that the disciplines differ substantially in their reliance on nominal elaboration, although the magnitude of the difference varies across sub-genres.
The writers who contributed to the volume were asked to describe their methods in detail.
The framework which they propose has been widely adopted in subsequent work.
These findings suggest that disciplinary variation should be modelled as a continuum rather than as a set of discrete categories.
Understanding the interaction between discipline and communicative purpose therefore requires a more explicit account of the mechanisms involved.
The analysis reported here is necessarily limited by the size of the sample, and further investigation is required.
