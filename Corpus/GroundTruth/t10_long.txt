The relationship between grammatical complexity and register has been the subject of considerable discussion in corpus linguistics.
Earlier accounts tended to treat complexity as a property of sentences, measured by the number of clauses that a sentence contains.
More recent work has argued that complexity is better understood as a property of the noun phrase, and that the two kinds of complexity are distributed differently across registers.
Conversational texts, for example, tend to rely on clause combination, whereas academic texts rely heavily on phrasal elaboration.
This distinction has consequences for the way in which complexity is measured, because measures designed for one kind of elaboration may systematically underestimate the other.
The present paper examines this issue using a corpus of published research articles and transcribed conversations.
Each text was annotated for syntactic structure, and a range of measures was computed at both the clause and the phrase level.
The results confirm that the two registers differ in the expected direction, but they also reveal considerable variation within each register.
Articles in the humanities, for instance, pattern more like conversation than articles in the physical sciences do.
These findings suggest that register differences are gradient rather than categorical, and that the notion of a single complexity score is difficult to justify.
A more defensible approach is to report several measures and to interpret them in the light of the communicative purposes that characterise each register.
Such an approach requires, however, that the measures themselves be clearly defined and consistently applied.
The discussion concludes by proposing a small set of measures that appear to capture the relevant distinctions without imposing an unwarranted uniformity on the data.
Further work will be needed to establish whether these measures behave similarly in other languages and in other kinds of specialised discourse.
Two limitations of the present study should be acknowledged at the outset.
First, the corpus is relatively small, and the genres that it represents are not evenly distributed across the sample.
Second, the annotation scheme was developed for written English, and its application to transcribed speech required several adjustments that may have introduced inconsistency.
Neither limitation, however, affects the central claim, which is that clause-level and phrase-level complexity must be distinguished if register differences are to be described adequately.
The measures proposed here are intended as a starting point rather than as a definitive solution, and they should be refined in the light of further evidence.
